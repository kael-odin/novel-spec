She burned on the third night.

Aren changed the wet cloth three times. Water boiled on the stove. She lay
curled in the wool, her lips blistered, speaking in broken pieces. Grey Harbor,
first, twice. Then Mira — a name he knew the moment he heard it, the way a hand
knows a key in the dark. He stood at the bedside with the cloth raised and
listened to a fever speak his daughter's name.

After midnight she called out for the light. "The light is turning." She said it
to the ceiling, as if the ceiling were the sea. He understood: she had come by
the beam. The boat had broken on the rocks and she had floated the last of the
water, and the light had turned and turned and brought her to the beach.

The fever broke at dawn, the way a tide goes out. She opened her eyes, looked
at the ceiling, then at his hands.

"Water."

He gave it to her. She held the cup a moment before she spoke. "You never asked
my name."

"You never gave it."

"Corra."

The name dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Four years ago, Mira
had stood in that doorway, pack on her shoulder. He had asked who she was going
to find. Corra, she said. He had thought it a dead name. Corra's boat had gone
out in the first winter after the Blackout and the whole coast knew that boat.

"She said she was going to find Corra," Aren said. "I thought she made it up."

"She didn't." Corra said it like a fact, not a defense. "She found me. I was
cleaning fish on the Grey Harbor dock. She walked four years and asked at every
port, and she found me."

Aren said nothing. He looked at the watch on the table. He had put it there
while he kept the vigil, knowing it was stopped, looking at it anyway.

"That's it," Corra said. "Mira told me about it. She said you take it out every
night."

He picked it up. Seven years he had carried it the way a man carries a scar.

"Mira said: tell him it isn't broken." Corra's voice went low, like a sentence
she had memorized. "It stopped at the moment the world stopped. Seven minutes
past three. She said time has been borrowed since that second. She doesn't
trust clocks anymore. She keeps time by the tides and the shadows."

He did not put the watch back in his pocket. He carried it down the stairs.

The archive door was heavy. The brass key turned once, and he spread the
pre-Blackout coast chart on the table, its edges gone brittle, its ink faded to
pale blue. He found the river mouth. His finger stopped there.

Grey Harbor. On paper. Older than his watch, older than the Blackout.

Paper does not lie. Paper only ages. He folded the chart along its oldest
crease and carried it up.

Corra watched him from the pillow. He laid the chart on her knees.

"It's there," he said.

She did not answer. Her finger followed the river line and rested on the ink.

He went to the window. The dock was below, his boat tied in the water, the
knots his own. Every autumn by now he had hauled it up, blocked it, and waited
out the winter. This year he did not move it.

Outside, the beam turned, swept the water, turned again. Corra said from the
bed, "Nine days. Maybe ten."

He did not turn around.
