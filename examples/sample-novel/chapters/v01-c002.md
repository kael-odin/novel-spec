She woke on the second day and asked for water before she asked his name.

"The note," she said, when she could speak. "You have to read the note."

Aren held the cup to her lips. "You've been half-dead for thirty hours. The note
can wait another thirty."

"It can't." She tried to sit up and the room tilted. He caught her shoulder. "I
promised. I promised her I'd put it in your hands, and I don't know how many days
I have left, and the storms —" She stopped, counting something. "Twelve days.
Maybe eleven. Then the island seals."

He did not ask how she knew his name, or his island. Strangers who wash up with
sealed notes are not strangers; they are messages wearing a body. He had learned
that much from the years of silence.

He took the paper. The waxed cloth came away stiff. Inside was a single sheet,
and on it, in a hand he would have known in the dark —

He stopped.

"Grey Harbor," the stranger said, gently, as if she had been waiting for him to
arrive at this particular silence. "She's in Grey Harbor. She's alive."

The watch in his pocket, stopped at seven past three, felt suddenly heavier than
it had in seven years. He had carried it believing it marked an ending. The
handwriting in his hands said otherwise.

"Why didn't she come herself?"

The stranger looked at the window, where the sleet had started again. "She
tried," she said. "Three times. The boats came back without her. The fourth time
she sent me." A pause. "I don't know why she couldn't come. She didn't tell me
that part."

Outside, the beam turned, and turned, and turned. In two weeks the storms would
close the coast. And Aren had a decision to make that he had not expected to
survive long enough to make.
